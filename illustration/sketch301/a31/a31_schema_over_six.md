# Sketch 301 — Assignment 31: Five-Slot Schema Over Six Elements

The risky test, self-assigned per the A30 verdict: a die-five quincunx (a five-slot
schema) plus a SIXTH dot. The roster-match account makes a hard prediction — the
schema preserves its roster and the count stays five. The professor's "fewer degrees
of freedom" reading and the A29 field account predict the schema only deletes an
ABSORBABLE extra; a salient sixth should survive. The sixth was dosed across that
exact axis so one of the two readings has to break.

## Materials

Fixed strong quincunx (4 corners + center) in every image; the sixth dot placed at
four increasing saliences, kept off the die grid lines so it cannot complete a
die-six:

- **a — interior:** tucked between center and top-left corner (absorbable).
- **b — midway:** interior, just above the center (inside the square).
- **c — edge:** just below the die, outside the square (salient).
- **d — far:** far right, a clearly detached outlier (most salient).

Fresh viewers, 1 Sonnet + 1 Haiku per image = 8 sessions. "What is it?" only.
Actual count is SIX in every image.

## Observations

1. **The hard prediction held on COUNT — and it can be stated as a win because it
   could have lost.** Wherever the Sonnet recruited the quincunx (a, b, c), it
   reported FIVE over a six-element page: the schema preserved its roster and
   sacrificed an element, exactly as roster-match forecast. This is the first
   schema-driven UNDER-count produced on demand, and it is the prediction the A30
   verdict demanded — a forecast that could fail. It did not fail where the schema
   was recruited.

2. **But the prediction was incomplete, and the gap is the result.** Roster-match
   says the count is five; it does NOT say WHICH element dies. The data answer that
   separately, and they answer it the professor's way: when the sixth was absorbable
   (interior), the SIXTH was deleted; when the sixth was salient (midway, edge), the
   sixth SURVIVED and a CORNER was deleted instead. **The schema fixes the number;
   salience picks the victim.** Both readings were partly right — the count obeys
   the roster (my account), the deletion lands at the least-guarded site (the A29
   field account, the professor's "degrees of freedom"). A31 doesn't break one; it
   shows they govern different variables.

3. **The schema BROKE at maximum salience (d).** The far outlier was not deleted and
   not seated — the Sonnet abandoned the quincunx entirely and read a six-dot
   "textile pattern." There is a salience past which the schema is not recruited at
   all, and then the roster cannot be enforced because there is no roster. Schema
   protection has a breaking load.

4. **The cohort lock did exactly what the roster account predicts.** The Haiku, which
   files genre and never recruits the die-schema, counted SIX in all four — correct,
   because it ran no five-roster to enforce. The over/under-count is not a property
   of the images; it is a property of whether a viewer recruits a roster-bearing
   schema. Same eight images, one cohort distorts toward its schema, one reports the
   page. This is the cleanest cohort dissociation in the series: the count diverges
   exactly where one cohort recruits a roster and the other does not.

5. **The whole census arc now has a clean two-factor statement** (offered, per the
   A30 warning that the one-factor version explained too much): **a recruited
   roster-bearing schema sets the COUNT to its roster; the FIELD's attention
   landscape sets which element absorbs the difference.** Roster without a misfit →
   correct (A30 die-five over five). Roster over a surplus → under-count, victim at
   the quiet site (A31 a/b/c). Roster over a deficit → phantom, invented at the
   quiet site (A21/A25/A29 +1). No roster recruited → the page is reported (Haiku
   throughout). The two factors are now separable because A31 moved them in
   opposite directions and watched them act independently.

## Explanations (held separately, tentative)

- The factorization survives A31 because A31 was built to break it and instead split
  it cleanly. It is no longer "explains everything" in the bad sense — it now makes
  a DOUBLE prediction (count AND victim-location) and both halves were checked
  against the salience dose. The remaining risk: it has only been run where the
  roster is five; a non-five roster-schema (a recognizable SEVEN-layout, a domino)
  over a mismatched field would test transfer.
- Why the schema breaks at d and not c: the edge sixth still sits within the die's
  bounding region (below-center reads as "under the five"); the far sixth is outside
  any frame the quincunx defines, so it competes as a separate object rather than an
  intruder into the schema's territory. Recruitment may require the surplus to fall
  inside the schema's catchment. (Boundary geometry; one image each side.)

## Open questions

- Transfer: a roster ≠ 5 schema (domino-six = 6 slots) laid over 5 or 7 elements —
  does it invent/delete to its OWN roster? (The factorization's strongest remaining
  risk.)
- The victim rule, dosed: between interior (sixth dies) and midway (corner dies)
  there is a crossover — at what sixth-salience does the deletion jump from the
  sixth to a corner? (Locates the "least-guarded site" quantitatively.)
- The breaking load (c vs d): is schema-recruitment gated by the surplus falling
  inside the schema's bounding region? A sixth at the square's edge vs just past it.
- Does the Haiku EVER recruit a roster-schema (the die-five at A30-c, it called
  "almost face-like")? A cohort that never enforces a roster would make "recruits a
  roster" the whole story — worth a targeted die-five-only Haiku cell.

*No findings. The schema kept its five and threw an element overboard — the absorbable
one when it could, a corner when the sixth refused to drown, and the whole boat when
the sixth stood too far off to be crew.*

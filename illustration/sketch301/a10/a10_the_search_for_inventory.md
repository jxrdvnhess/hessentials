# Sketch 301 — Assignment 10 (self-assigned): The Search for Inventory

Per the A9 review: don't search for absence — search for inventory. The question moved
upstream: **what tells a viewer what ought to be there?** Test: structures that declare
their own contents wordlessly, each shown minus one member, against complete controls.

## Method

Three inventory-declaring mechanisms, chosen for increasing declarative force:

- **Series** — a clock face: twelve numbered positions, each member NAMING its own slot.
  Test: the 7 absent (its tick mark retained). Control: all twelve.
- **Lattice** — a shelf row: six evenly spaced slots, five bottles, slot four empty,
  spacing preserved so the slot is visible. Control: five bottles, even, no gap.
- **Symmetry** — a moth: bilateral structure declaring its own other half. Test: right
  wing absent entirely. Control: both wings.

Scoring pre-defined as in A9: absence-experienced ("missing," "absent," "empty slot,"
"one wing") vs completed/converted. 9 test sessions + 6 control sessions, fresh viewers
(Sonnet/Haiku), neutral filenames, "What is it?" only.

## Results

**Test sessions: 9 of 9 completed. Zero absence-experienced.** Controls all read
normally.

### Series — the clock missing its 7

All three viewers read a normal clock. Two asserted the inventory as PRESENT:
"hour numerals **1 through 12** positioned around the perimeter" / "hour numbers
**(1 through 12)** arranged around the dial." The third described "plain numerals with
simple tick marks." Nobody reported the empty position. The strongest declared
inventory in the set produced not absence-experience but **asserted presence of the
absent member** — the viewers reported the category's inventory, not the image's.

### Lattice — the shelf with an empty slot

All three viewers SAW the gap. All three filed it as arrangement: "a small gap between
the third and fourth bottles, **loosely dividing them into a group of three and a group
of two**" / "three grouped on the left, a gap in the middle, and two on the right...
**collection display**" / "**two loose groupings**." The slot reached every description
and was employed as composition in every one. No empty slot, no missing bottle.

### Symmetry — the moth with one wing

All three viewers reported a complete butterfly/moth. The asymmetry was seen and
attributed to the maker: "wings... arranged **asymmetrically**" as a design property /
"a slightly asymmetric, **hand-constructed feeling**" / "wings" (plural) "almost
**collage-like**." Bilateral inventory — the body and antennae sit on the midline,
declaring the other half — produced zero reports of a missing wing. The hand absorbed
it (A9 Obs 4's legalizer, replicated on a new object and a structural deletion).

## Observations

1. **Declared inventory did not produce experienced absence — 9 of 9.** The preserved
   question gets its first clean answer-shaped pressure: inventory, at least as
   declared by series, lattice, and symmetry, is NOT SUFFICIENT. The clock is the
   sharpest case on the board: the inventory declaration HELPED the completion — the
   category "clock" carries "1–12" as furniture (A1 Obs 11), and the viewers reported
   the furniture over the image. Declaring contents through a category hands the
   completion machinery a script.

2. **Seen-not-missed replicated twice more** (A9 Obs 6, elevated last review): the
   shelf gap and the moth's asymmetry both reached description — as grouping and as
   style — and neither was ever missed as contents. Three assignments, three
   structures, same event: the gap is perceived, then employed.

3. **The attribution machinery handled all three mechanisms with its existing
   routes.** Series → category-script ("1 through 12"); lattice → arrangement
   ("groupings"); symmetry → the maker's hand ("hand-constructed"). No new completion
   route was needed; A9's taxonomy absorbed A10's structures without strain.

4. **A confound, owned, that is also the phenomenon:** the clock viewers who asserted
   "1 through 12" may never have inspected the numerals individually — the category
   answered before inspection. This cannot be separated from "inspected and
   completed" in this design. But that inseparability is itself the course's oldest
   observation wearing new clothes: the answer arrives before the looking finishes
   (A1, A5), and asserts what the looking would have disconfirmed.

5. **Contrast that finally discriminates** (across assignments, observation-grade):
   every absence-adjacent event the course has produced — A7-P1b's "too small to read
   clearly," A5-n4's candidate trough — involved marks PRESENT and unresolvable.
   Every failure to experience absence — A8's heads, A9's blades, A10's 7, slot, and
   wing — involved marks ABSENT at the lacuna. Nothing present has ever gone
   unexplained; nothing absent has ever been missed.

## Explanations (held separately, tentative)

- Experienced absence may require a RESISTING PRESENCE, not a declared inventory: a
  thing in the image that is seen and refuses resolution. You cannot trouble a viewer
  with what isn't there; the completion machinery owns all vacancies. You may be able
  to trouble them with what IS there and won't close. If so, the craft target was
  never "make them feel something missing" but "give them something present that
  cannot be filed" — and the inventory question collapses into the older question of
  what resists filing. (One course of evidence, all single-cohort; held as
  explanation.)
- The clock suggests a corollary: category-declared inventory actively IMMUNIZES
  against absence — the stronger the category's standard contents, the more confidently
  the contents are reported regardless of the instance. The place absence cannot hide
  is exactly where everyone knows what belongs.

## Open questions

- Does a resisting presence at the lacuna change the result? One cell would test it:
  the clock with its 7 replaced by an unresolvable mark (a smudge, a wrong glyph, a
  rotated 7) rather than nothing. If viewers report THAT, presence-that-resists beats
  inventory. Not run — next round's first candidate.
- Is there any deletion a category-script cannot paper over? (Remove THREE numerals?
  Remove the hands? At some point the clock must stop being a clock — is that
  identity-loss or finally absence?)
- The moth's missing wing was absorbed by the hand. Would the same deletion in a
  photographic register — where the hand is unavailable as author — survive
  attribution? (A9's open question, now sharper, still untested.)
- The human eye: Jordan would presumably see the missing 7 instantly — or would he?
  Viewer-relativity (A7 Obs 2) has never been tested on absence. A one-image human
  session is cheap and would say much.

*No findings. Nine completions, an inventory hypothesis weakened by its own test, and
one discriminating contrast carried forward.*

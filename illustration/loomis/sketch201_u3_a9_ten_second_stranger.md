# Sketch 201 · Unit 3 · A9 — The Ten-Second Stranger

**Method (stated plainly, because the assignment is about honesty).** As an AI I cannot make
an image "disappear" from my context the way a person looks away — so to run the experiment
honestly I encoded the stranger *once, at a small gist-level size* (a glance, not a study),
then wrote the fifty statements below from that impression without re-opening it. Only after
the fifty were locked did I open the photograph at full resolution to answer the three
questions. The fifty are memory; the comparison is what re-looking revealed.

Statements are ordered roughly by how strongly they survived — earliest = clearest. No
hedging words. Where I retained nothing, there is no statement; the gaps are reported in
Question 2.

## Question 1 — What survived?

The **composition and the figure-to-room relationship** survived almost intact. Centred figure;
large gridded industrial window filling the wall behind him; daylight from behind; dark figure
read against bright glass; bare space with him small inside it; high contrast; near-symmetry.
Every one of those was accurate.

The **gross posture** survived: seated, leaning forward off the backrest, hands brought together
low, facing the camera, compact and contained, still. Accurate.

The **tonal relationships** survived: dark upper half, lighter legs, dark shoes, dark hair, and a
single warm accent at the chest standing against the dark jacket. Accurate.

What survived, in one line: *where a dark shape sat in a bright room, and how it was arranged.*

## Question 2 — What disappeared?

- **His face — entirely.** Features, expression, age. I left this gap empty, and the gap was total.
- **The garment identity.** I kept "a warm accent at the chest" as a tone; I lost that it is a
  cream cardigan layered under the jacket over a pale top. Memory kept the tonal relationship and
  discarded the object.
- **The chair.** I kept "a chair." It is a swivel office chair with a wheeled base — none of that survived.
- **The exact set of the lean.** It is an elbows-on-thighs, hands-clasped-between-the-knees forward
  set; I softened it to "hands in his lap."
- **Local specifics:** the exposed-brick right wall and pipe (I kept only "industrial/bare"); the
  concrete floor; whether anything is held in the clasped hands; his build and proportions. All gaps.
- **One colour drift:** the legs are light blue denim; I recorded "grey."

## Question 3 — What surprised me about the difference?

Three things, smallest to largest.

First, I remembered the **room better than the man.** Asked to remember a stranger, what I actually
kept was a setting with a dark shape placed in it. The person — the supposed subject — is the part
that vanished.

Second, memory kept **relationships and discarded identities.** "Warm accent against a dark jacket"
survived; "cream cardigan" did not. "Dark against bright" survived; his face did not. The thing that
lasted was never an object — it was always a relation between things.

Third, and this is the one that matters: **everywhere I had no memory I left a gap — except once,
where the mind quietly filled it and lied.** I wrote "his knees are close together" (statement 21).
His knees are apart, with the clasped hands between them. I did not retain knee-spacing, so the mind
supplied its default and handed it to me as recall. Every other gap stayed honest; this one
negotiated. That single substitution is the whole lesson of the assignment in miniature — the
invention does not announce itself, it arrives wearing the same voice as the memory. Catching it is
the only defence.

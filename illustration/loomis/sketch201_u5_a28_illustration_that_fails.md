# Sketch 201 · Unit 5 · A28 — The Illustration That Fails

Same article: **"What the Cows Know"** (`content/practice/what-the-cows-know.md`). Three
illustrations that would be attractive, clever, and publishable — and wrong. Each is a real
temptation, not a strawman. For each: why an illustrator would choose it, what residue it
ignores, and what it explains that the article never actually shows. (Residue established in A27:
one-minute → cows + body-knowledge + examples; one-hour → cows + "the body knows first"; next-day
→ the field of cows lying down.)

---

## Fail 1 — The head-and-gut duality

*The image:* a single figure rendered with two centres of knowing — the head one way, the gut or
chest another; or a head split into a deliberating half and a sensing half.

**1. Why an illustrator would choose it.** The article hands it over directly: "There are two ways
of knowing. One happens in your head. The other happens lower and faster" (line 15). A clean
two-system duality is instantly legible, conceptually tidy, and very publishable — editorial work
loves a crisp diagram of an idea.

**2. What residue it ignores.** All of it. No duration's residue is a divided body — not at a
minute, not at an hour, not the next day. It discards the one image that survives (the lying-down
herd) in favor of a concept the reader does not actually carry away.

**3. What it explains that the article never shows.** It diagrams the *mechanism*. The article is
emphatic that there is "nothing mystical about this… not a sixth sense. It's the first five,
working at a speed thinking can't match" (line 27). A glowing-gut duality re-mystifies exactly what
the text de-mystifies, and it depicts a split self the article never shows — it illustrates the
explanation, not the thing.

---

## Fail 2 — The charged doorway

*The image:* a person stopped at a threshold, the room ahead heavy with something wrong — a tense,
cinematic "your body knew before you did" moment.

**1. Why an illustrator would choose it.** The article's most vivid, relatable lines are here: "You
walk into a room and something is wrong… you read a message and your stomach drops" (lines 15–17).
They are cinematic and human; an illustrator would dramatize that charged instant for immediacy and
atmosphere.

**2. What residue it ignores.** This is the one-minute residue — a mid-article example among several
— and per A27 it is gone by the next day. It serves only the glance and leaves nothing for the
reader who carries the piece; it ignores the durable cows.

**3. What it explains that the article never shows.** It stages a threat. The article specifically
withholds drama: "most of the time, the override doesn't produce catastrophe… nothing dramatic
occurred" (line 21). A menacing doorway invents the peril the text says usually isn't there,
explaining intuition as danger-detection theatre — a register the quiet piece refuses.

---

## Fail 3 — The birds fleeing the storm

*The image:* a darkening sky, a flock streaming away ahead of a hurricane — dynamic, dramatic,
photogenic.

**1. Why an illustrator would choose it.** It is the most visually exciting image the article
offers — "Birds do the same thing before hurricanes. They leave days in advance" (line 13) — and it
rhymes with the cows while being far more kinetic and beautiful on a page. Easy to want.

**2. What residue it ignores.** The birds are a single supporting line, not the bookended image.
The residue that survives is the cows (open, line 9; close, lines 33–35), not the birds. This swaps
the persistent image for the flashier instance.

**3. What it explains that the article never shows.** It dramatizes the *weather* — the disaster,
the storm — which A27 already flagged as the article's explanation of what the animals sense, not
its residue. The article never shows a storm; it shows a herd lying calmly in grass (a quiet,
readable sign). Birds-fleeing-catastrophe imports spectacle and an apocalyptic register the piece
deliberately avoids.

---

## What the three failures share

None of them is bad work — each is legible, atmospheric, or striking, which is exactly the danger.
What they have in common is that they **explain or dramatize** rather than show: the duality
diagrams the idea, the doorway stages the threat, the birds perform the disaster. Each reaches for
the article's *argument* or its *peril*. The image that survives reaches for neither — it is the
plain observed thing the article keeps returning to. The dangerous illustration is the one that
illustrates the meaning; the durable one illustrates what was seen. (Stated as the pattern across
these three, not as a law.)

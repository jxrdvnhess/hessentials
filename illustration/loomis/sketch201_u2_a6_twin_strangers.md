# Sketch 201 · Unit 2 · A6 — Twin Strangers

Two young men who, at thumbnail size, feel interchangeable: similar age, same sex, slim
build, dark hair, dark layered casual streetwear, candid outdoors. No drawing, no rig, no
construction. Only evidence — and, where possible, relationships rather than measurements.
The enemy here is assumption: the category that makes a stranger disappear.

- **Person A — wassim** (black puffer over hoodie, standing on a crosswalk)
- **Person B — nima** (olive quilted vest over a pale shirt, mid-stride on a walkway)

## Part 1 — Why I first believed these were the same kind of person

The assumption was built from a short list of shared facts, read fast: both are young
men of about the same age; both are slim; both have dark hair; both wear a dark, muted
outer layer left open over an inner top — a puffer over a hoodie on one, a vest over a
shirt on the other, but at a glance the same *structure*: open shell, visible layer
beneath. Both wear slim trousers and casual shoes. Both were caught candid, outdoors, in
a plain urban setting, with the hands carried low toward the front. At thumbnail size every
one of those facts collapses into a single label — "young man, dark layers, slim, outside" —
and the label does the recognizing instead of the eye. That is the assumption: the category
arrives first, and once it arrives it stops me looking. The two men become one type with
two photographs.

## Part 2 — Why they are not the same

The evidence pulls them apart at the very place the category was blindest: **where the head
lives relative to the body.** Person A's head hangs forward and down, chin buried in the
collar, the front of the neck closed, the whole upper body curling inward over a settled,
static weight — an axis off plumb. Person B's head is up and level, stacked over the chest,
the full neck exposed, the trunk upright and moving forward over a planted stride — an axis
near plumb. One collapses; the other carries. That single relationship would separate them
across a dark room.

The head itself is a different object: A's hair is full and curled, wider than the skull,
hairline low, ears covered — a large soft head-mass; B's hair is cropped close to the skull,
hairline high, ears and jaw clear — a small hard head-mass. And the "same" outer layer is in
fact opposite in what it does: A's padded jacket sits *outboard* of the real shoulders and
swallows the arms, so the true frame is hidden and the body reads broad and soft up top;
B's fitted vest sits *on* the real shoulder joint and leaves the shirt-sleeved arms showing,
so the true slim frame is visible. The arms confirm it — A's hang low and close, swallowed;
B's are in a stride swing, one gap open behind, one closed in front.

So the shared facts were all surface (age, slimness, dark layered clothing, setting). The
differences are structural and relational: head-over-chest versus head-ahead-of-chest,
upright-and-moving versus settled-and-curling, hard small head versus soft large head, frame
revealed versus frame hidden. The assumption made two strangers into one type; the evidence
makes them two people again. That is the whole danger — the category is faster than the eye,
and it is usually wrong.

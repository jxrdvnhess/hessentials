# Sketch 201 · Unit 2 · A8 — The Same Room

Three people, the same type of space: a sunlit, plant-touched interior, each seated alone
and attending to something in front of them. No drawing, no rig, no narrative invention.
The observations stay on the evidence — where the person sits in the room, what they face,
how much space they take and how much they leave — so that the reading of *presence* is
forced by the evidence rather than narrated onto it.

- **Person 1 — sweet** (older, laptop, long table along a window wall)
- **Person 2 — michael** (armchair by a tall window, large plant beside him)
- **Person 3 — jamie** (phone, dense plant corner, hard dappled sun)

---

## Person 1 — thirty observations

1. He sits at the far end of a long table that runs the length of a window wall.
2. The table stretches away empty to one side; he uses only its end.
3. Both forearms come down onto the surface, anchoring him to the table.
4. He leans in toward the laptop, closing the gap between face and screen.
5. The open laptop is the one lit, attended object in a wide bright field.
6. Daylight floods the room from the windows, but his gaze stays down on the screen.
7. He faces along the table, not out to the bright glass beside him.
8. His back curves forward over the keys, shoulders rolling in toward the work.
9. The room's brightness continues past him, unclaimed; he occupies one pool of it.
10. The cleared table around the laptop shows a single working spot, not a spread.
11. His elbows stay close to his sides, the activity kept compact.
12. Both hands rest on the keys; the room's stillness gathers at his fingertips.
13. He reads as the only active thing in an otherwise still, sunlit room.
14. The foliage beyond the glass is bright but behind him, outside his attention.
15. His head sits lowest of the three, dropped toward the work surface.
16. The chair tucks him close to the table — no recline, no gap at his back.
17. He has turned a long, almost communal table into a desk for one.
18. The light falls across the table toward him, yet he works against it, into the screen.
19. Nothing decorative sits within reach; the surface is functional and cleared.
20. His presence concentrates at the table's end, leaving its length open and empty.
21. The posture pulls the eye to the small bright rectangle of the screen.
22. He occupies the room narrowly — a column of attention from head to hands to laptop.
23. The room's airy volume is set aside in favour of a screen's width of space.
24. His stillness is held over a task, not at rest.
25. The room reads calm while he reads concentrated — a contrast, not a match.
26. The window sits at his shoulder; he uses its light without looking up into it.
27. The empty length of table makes him look solitary where he sits.
28. His occupancy gives the bright room a single point of purpose.
29. He faces neither the view nor the door — only the work.
30. He makes the room a workplace at one end while the rest stays a sunroom.

## Person 2 — thirty observations

1. He sits in a single armchair set close to a tall bright window.
2. One ankle crosses onto the opposite knee, opening his outline into the room.
3. He leans back into the chair, giving it his weight.
4. His face turns away from the camera, toward the window light.
5. His gaze travels outward and up to the glass, not down to any object.
6. His hands rest quietly; nothing is held or worked.
7. A potted plant nearly his own height stands beside him, sharing the corner.
8. Wide areas of floor and wall sit empty around the chair.
9. He leaves that emptiness intact, occupying only the chair and its near space.
10. The warm wall and window frame him without crowding him.
11. His outline is open — crossed leg out, arm along the rest — claiming a relaxed width.
12. The light comes from in front; he faces it rather than turning his back to it.
13. He sits low and settled, the chair a calm anchor in the room.
14. The tall plant and the seated man balance across the corner as two upright forms.
15. Air stands above and around his head; the room's volume stays open.
16. His stillness is restful — no task pulls him forward.
17. The eye moves from him outward to the window, following his own gaze.
18. He occupies the room widely but lightly — present, not pressing.
19. His pale shoes catch the light, set out onto the open floor.
20. Nothing within reach is a tool; the near objects are a plant and a stand.
21. He treats the chair as a place to sit *with* the room, not to work in it.
22. The window's brightness is the room's centre, and he is turned toward it.
23. His relaxed diagonal — crossed leg, leaned torso — loosens the room's geometry.
24. He neither hides in a corner nor centres himself; he sits at ease, off to one side.
25. The room keeps its calm; his stillness confirms rather than disturbs it.
26. He shares the corner with the plant as an equal occupant, not a user of furniture.
27. His outward gaze opens the room toward what lies beyond the glass.
28. The emptiness around him reads as chosen calm, not absence.
29. He gives the bright room a quiet, contemplative centre.
30. His presence lets the room stay a room — open, lit, unhurried.

## Person 3 — thirty observations

1. He sits hemmed in by dense potted plants on one side and a hanging plant above.
2. The greenery presses close to his head and shoulder, narrowing his space.
3. He bows forward over a phone held in both hands, head dropped toward it.
4. His shoulders round inward, closing the body around the small lit object.
5. Sun cuts across him in hard dappled patches, broken by leaves.
6. His attention drops entirely to his hands; the busy room is unregarded.
7. The space is cluttered and warm, every margin filled with leaf, pot, or glow.
8. He fits himself into a gap among the plants rather than onto open floor.
9. His dark clothing sinks into the shadowed, leafy surround.
10. The phone is the one cool bright point in a warm, dense field.
11. His elbows draw in to his ribs; the pose is compact and private.
12. The plants lean toward him until he and the foliage interlock into one mass.
13. Little empty space surrounds him — the room closes in at arm's length.
14. His bowed head and curved back make a small enclosed shape within the clutter.
15. Warm light and tight framing wrap him in an intimate pocket.
16. He faces neither window nor room, only the cradle of his own hands.
17. The density of objects makes the corner feel occupied even before him.
18. He adds to that density, becoming one more warm form among many.
19. His stillness is absorbed and inward, sealed off from the room.
20. The eye is pulled down to his hands, then held by the surrounding tangle.
21. He takes up little width but anchors the busy corner's centre.
22. The plants at his shoulder read almost as company, crowding companionably.
23. No clear floor or wall frames him; texture surrounds him on all sides.
24. His presence deepens the corner's enclosure rather than opening it.
25. The space is the warmest and most cluttered of the three, and he folds in to match it.
26. He has chosen the densest nook, not an open seat, to occupy.
27. His downward focus makes the cluttered corner feel hushed.
28. The room and the man share one temperature — warm, close, absorbed.
29. He gives the cluttered corner a still, private centre.
30. His presence turns a busy cafe corner into a cocoon.

## How does a person change a room simply by being inside it?

None of the three moved any furniture. The room type is the same for all of them — a
sunlit interior with plants, a seat, something in front of them to attend to. And yet each
is plainly a different room, because the occupant decides, just by sitting, four things the
walls cannot decide for themselves: **where the room's centre is, how fast time moves in it,
how warm it is, and what it is for.**

Person 1 drops his attention onto a screen and pulls a wide bright space down to one working
rectangle at the end of a long table. He leaves most of the room unclaimed, so the room
keeps its light — but where he sits, it becomes a workplace, and time there is busy. Person 2
leans back and sends his gaze out the window, hands empty, leaving floor and air open around
him. He gives the room repose; its centre becomes the bright glass he faces, and time there
slows to stillness. Person 3 bows into the densest plant corner over his phone and folds
inward until he and the clutter read as one warm mass. He gives the room enclosure; its
centre contracts to the cradle of his hands, and time there goes quiet and private.

So presence is not what a person does *to* a room — it is the field the body throws over the
space: where it pulls the eye, whether it opens the volume or closes it, and what purpose it
silently assigns. The same light, the same plants, the same chair-by-a-window become a
workplace, a place of repose, and a refuge, because three different people decided what the
room was about without touching a thing in it. That is why presence is foundational to
editorial illustration: a figure placed in a scene does not merely sit there — it tells the
reader what the scene is *for*.

# Sketch 201 · Unit 5 · A43 — The First Lie

Start from the coalition calf — which works — and add five plausible, well-intentioned marks that
each *secretly* damage it. Each should first read as "more information," then resolve as "less
clarity." For each: the promise it made, the damage it did, and why the damage was easy to miss.

## The five lies

**Second ground line** — a faint horizontal behind the calf.
- *Promise:* depth. A far field-edge; the calf now sits in a place, not a void.
- *Damage:* it runs across the calf's lower body, so it's no longer clear whether the animal is in
front of or behind that edge. The single ground line had given one clean contact; now there are two
candidate grounds and the calf hangs between them. The resting read — the one thing the drawing was
about — loosens.
- *Why missed:* a second horizontal reads as competent landscape before you notice it slices the
subject. Depth is a known virtue, so the eye grants it credit on sight.

**Texture patch (spots)** — two filled dark blobs under the topline.
- *Promise:* specificity. Hide markings; *clearly* a cow now, not just a line.
- *Damage:* the blobs are solid fill — heavier than any line in the drawing — so attention leaves the
animal and lands on the spots. They also sit near the hump, the one dark mass that was doing
structural work, and dilute it: two dark masses, neither decisive.
- *Why missed:* spots feel like added information, and information feels like a gain. The damage is a
hierarchy problem — what the eye goes to first — which you don't see until you stop reading the mark
and watch where you look.

**Interior fold** — a vertical contour from topline through the belly.
- *Promise:* anatomy. A flank fold, a plane, three dimensions instead of a flat outline.
- *Damage:* it reads as a *second edge* cutting the body into two — a seam, a far leg, or the line
where two animals overlap. It asserts a boundary the silhouette never claimed and can't support (the
A42 shoulder-line lesson, but here it actively severs rather than merely confuses).
- *Why missed:* an interior anatomy line looks knowledgeable; it signals that the maker knows what's
under the skin. Intention reads as expertise, so the line is trusted before its effect is checked.

**Cast shadow** — a soft grey ellipse beneath the calf.
- *Promise:* grounding and light. The calf sits in real space under a real sun.
- *Damage:* the shape has no edge-logic tying it to the feet and no declared light direction, so it
reads as a puddle or a hole as readily as a shadow. It adds a second large mass competing with the
figure; the calf can look like it floats above or sinks into it.
- *Why missed:* shadows are conventionally virtuous — everyone adds them, grounding is a known good —
so the mark borrows the convention's credit and skips the question of whether *this* light was ever
established.

**Doubled back-line** — a faint line paralleling the topline.
- *Promise:* atmosphere. A soft, furry edge; air; something behind.
- *Damage:* the topline was the most load-bearing edge in the drawing — it carried the whole
silhouette read. Doubling it splits that authority: two candidate edges, neither definitive, reading
as a blur or a ghost animal behind.
- *Why missed:* a soft second line feels like rendering, like painterly sophistication. It looks like
craft being added, while it's actually the strongest line being weakened.

**All five** — the compound. The calf is barely legible: sliced by the second ground, severed by the
fold, outweighed by the spots and the shadow, doubled at the back. Death by helpful marks.

## What the run found

Why did they *seem* good? In this run, each lie borrowed credibility from a generic virtue — depth,
specificity, anatomy, grounding, atmosphere. Each is a thing it's normally good to add. The trap is
that the virtue is generic and the drawing is specific: the mark imports a good the drawing never
asked for and can't carry.

And the damage took one shape every time: the mark competed with, contradicted, or diluted a line
that was already doing a job. The spots fought the hump; the doubled line split the topline; the fold
contradicted the single volume; the second ground destabilized the one contact; the shadow added a
rival mass.

So the mark was easy to trust because it was judged by its *intention* — what it was trying to add —
read in isolation. The damage only appears in *relation*, against the marks already present. That is
the same relational truth this unit kept finding (a hump is only a hump relative to a back; a mark's
value is its standing in the coalition), turned around: the first lie is mistaking a mark's intention
for its effect. (Said of this build and these five marks — the mechanism I saw here, not a law of all
drawing.)

# Sketch 201 · Unit 5 · A21 — The Cost of Being Right

One photograph: a man seated in an armchair beside a tall bright window, his face and gaze
turned toward the glass; a large potted palm to his side, warm walls, open floor, a building
visible outside. Unit 5 rule observed — no beauty, no style. The question is not which reading
is correct, but what disappears when the correct one arrives. Evidence and its consequences only.

---

**1. The most accurate reading.** A man is seated, leaning back with his legs crossed, and his
face and eyes are directed toward the bright window beside him — he is looking toward the window
and the light. His hands are at rest; nothing is held or worked. Stated precisely, the image is a
figure turned to the window.

**2. A less accurate reading many viewers would still have.** A man sitting in his living room —
a person at home, present in his space, the room around him simply where he is. This read takes
in the whole interior at once and does not single out the window or the direction of his look; it
treats the man as one element inside a room that is the scene.

**3. What is lost when the accurate reading replaces the less accurate one.** The room as content.
The less accurate reading let the entire interior be the picture — the warm walls, the tall window
of light, the palm nearly his own height, the open floor — and that interior is genuinely most of
the frame. The accurate reading pulls everything onto a single line: the man's face to the window.
Once that vector is named, the rest of the room recedes to "around him" — a location for a glance
rather than the bulk of what is present. Being right replaces a *volume* (the whole room, which is
most of the image) with a *vector* (the gaze to the window), and everything in the volume that
does not sit on that vector — the plant, the walls, the floor, the open space — drops out of the
reading though it never left the frame.

---

**The cost.** Accuracy and completeness are not the same. The most accurate reading here is also
the narrowest: it is correct about the man and silent about the majority of the picture. The
looser reading was wrong to ignore the window, but it was faithful to how much of the frame is
room. Correctness, arriving, shrank the image to the one thing it could be precise about.

This is the mirror of A20. There, the loose reading was wrong to drop the small true thing (the
phone); here, the accurate reading is wrong to drop the large true thing (the room). Rushing has
a cost and so does correcting — both are failures of weighting, in opposite directions. Noted, not
built into a rule.

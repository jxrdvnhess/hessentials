# Sketch 201 · Unit 4 · A14 — The One Thing Missing

Three photographs. For each: the single element I would remove — not the person, not the
subject, not the largest object, but the unnoticed thing holding the frame together — then the
photograph after removal, then what became impossible once it was gone. Only evidence; no
symbolism, no psychology. Each removed element is a different *kind* of connector.

---

## 1 — Two old men on a harbour bench → remove the bench

**Removed:** the wooden bench (a shared support; neither man, neither the subject, nor the
largest thing in the frame — the harbour and the men are larger).

**After removal:** the two men hold their same seated shapes — knees bent, weight low, both
facing the water — but over nothing, at the harbour's edge, boats and masts unchanged behind.

**What became impossible:** reading them as a pair. The bench is the only object that touches
both men at once; it seats them at one height, in one line, facing one way. Remove it and there
is no single thing both occupy — they become two separate seated figures with nothing in the
frame held in common. The picture's "two men together" was carried entirely by the one seat
under both; without it the frame can no longer show them sharing anything.

---

## 2 — The man working in the sunlit room → remove the table

**Removed:** the long table (the surface the action rests on; not the man, not the work itself,
not the largest element — the windowed room is larger).

**After removal:** the man holds his forward-leaning posture — forearms out at keyboard height,
hands lowered, head dropped — but over empty air; the laptop and cleared surface gone; the
bright room unchanged around him.

**What became impossible:** the act of working. The whole posture is built on a horizontal
plane — forearms down onto it, hands at its level, torso tipped toward it. Remove the table and
the laptop has nowhere to sit and the hands rest on nothing; the lean becomes a man bent over
emptiness. The frame can no longer show "working at a desk," because every visible part of that
reading — the surface, the device, the supported hands — depended on the one table.

---

## 3 — The muscular man in the produce aisle → remove the produce shelves

**Removed:** the produce shelving and fruit bins behind and before him (the setting; not the
man, not the pineapple he attends to, and not the largest single object — the man is larger).

**After removal:** the braided man in his tank top, still raising the pineapple to eye level
and looking at it, now against a blank, placeless ground; the green rows and bins gone.

**What became impossible:** reading him as shopping. The pineapple reads as a grocery item only
because rows of produce surround it; the shelves and bins are the sole marks that this is a
place where fruit is chosen. Remove them and the identical gesture becomes a man holding a
pineapple nowhere in particular. The act of inspection survives — but "a shopper choosing fruit
he might buy" can no longer be shown, because nothing left in the frame marks the setting as a
store.

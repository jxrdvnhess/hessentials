# Sketch 201 · Unit 5 · A24 — The Thing Doing Two Jobs

One photograph: a man perched on a carved pale-stone block (a spiral-scrolled form) at the
lower-left, his body rising to the right against a plain sunlit wall. The element under study is
**the carved stone**. The task is labor, not meaning: name two functions it performs at once.
Evidence only; claims held to what the frame shows.

---

**1. The two jobs.**

- *Attractor.* The stone is the brightest, highest-contrast, and only carved form in the lower
half of the frame. In a picture otherwise made of a plain wall and a clothed figure, its lit
spiral is the one place of sculptural detail — a second pole the eye is drawn toward.

- *Structural support and counterweight.* The man sits on it; it sets his height and pose, and it
fills and weights the lower-left corner. The figure rises to the right, so the stone's mass on the
left balances him across the frame.

**2. How one element performs both.** Different properties do different work, and they coincide in
the one object. Its *surface* — bright, high-contrast, carved — does the attracting. Its *mass and
position* — load-bearing, lower-left, beneath the figure — do the supporting and the
counterweighting. The stone is simultaneously a bright thing the eye goes to and the base the
figure and the composition rest on.

**3. What breaks if only one job remains.**

- *Keep only the attractor* — a bright carved form, but not beneath him: the figure loses his
seat. The pose has no base, and the lower-left stops counterweighting; the balance tips toward the
right where the man is.

- *Keep only the support* — a plain, dull seat under him: the lower-left loses its second pole. The
eye stops dividing between man and stone, and the frame settles to a single pole on the figure (the
two-pole division of attention noted in A15 goes with it).

Either subtraction unbalances the frame in a different direction. The stone holds the picture
steady precisely because it does both jobs at once — a bright pole on the left and the base the
figure sits on — and the two are separable in principle though fused in this one object.

# Sketch 201 · Unit 5 · A46 — The Agreement Test

Self-assigned per "Proceed to A46": the A45 lead — *good marks create agreements* — is the strongest
on the board, so A46 tests it against accuracy. Four calves, the same ten marks each, on two axes:
**right/wrong** (true proportions vs a stretched, small-headed body, all junctions intact) and
**meeting/missing** (every junction lands vs every junction stops short or overshoots — each mark
individually plausible). Page: `loomis/sketch201_u5_a46_the_agreement_test.png` (generator
`_a46_the_agreement_test.py`). Run, then looked.

## What the page shows

Strongest: right and meeting. No contest.

The informative pair is the diagonal: **wrong-meeting reads as one animal that may not be a calf;
right-missing reads as a calf that may not be one drawing.** The stretched, connected version stays
a single contained thing — its wrongness reads as a different (or stylized) animal, not as failure.
The accurate, gapped version keeps its calf proportions, but the eye receives parts: a floating
topline, a muzzle drifting off the head, a hump beside its withers.

So the test split the question instead of answering it. On this page, meetings buy *unity* — that it
is one thing — and right marks buy *identity* — which thing it is. Agreement and accuracy are not
rivals for the same job; they pay different debts.

Two smaller observations. The eye forgave some misses — the hovering hooves and short throat read as
looseness, and the figure survived — so a miss is not priced flat. The expensive miss was at the
head: the detached muzzle broke the read worst, in the place attention goes (A42). A junction
appears to cost most where attention is highest.

The A45 lead survives, narrowed: agreements made the thing exist on every panel where they were
present, including the wrong one. (Said of these four panels and this calf — a split observed,
not a theory of drawing.)

# Sketch 201 · Unit 4 · A16 — The Image That Loses

Three photographs. For each: the strongest plausible subject, the second-strongest (a
genuinely strong runner-up, not a weak foil), and why the second loses. Ties are broken on
evidence inside the existing frame — orientation, dependence, internal form, discontinuity —
not on crop hypotheticals (a crop changes the evidence; it cannot prove what the given frame is
doing). Only what is visible. Selection here is elimination.

---

## 1 — The man in the produce aisle

**Strongest subject:** the man — the largest complete form, the only figure, the source of the
one action and the one gaze line in the frame.

**Runner-up (strong):** the pineapple — it is where his eyes and his only movement terminate;
A-grade attention is pointed straight at it; it is the focal object of the act.

**Why the pineapple loses:** dependence. Remove the man and the pineapple is anonymous produce
on a shelf — it carries nothing alone. Remove the pineapple and the man is still a complete
figure in the frame. The pineapple's entire claim to subjecthood is borrowed: it matters only
because a man is holding it to his eye. A subject can stand without its surroundings; the
pineapple cannot stand without the man. The thing attention lands on is not automatically the
subject — here it is downstream of the figure that activates it.

**Tie-breaker:** independence. The man survives the other's removal; the pineapple does not.

---

## 2 — The figure on the lakebank

**Strongest subject:** the reclining figure — propped on the elbows, lower-right, the only human
and the only oriented form in the frame.

**Runner-up (strong):** the lake and far treeline — they fill most of the frame by area and make
the image read, at a glance, as a landscape.

**Why the landscape loses:** structure. The water-and-trees is a broad, even, repeating texture
with no internal focal point — nowhere in it does the eye come to rest on its own. The figure is
the single discontinuity in that field, the one place edges and a clear form interrupt the green.
The eye resolves to it for lack of any competing focus, and the figure's orientation points *into*
the water, so the landscape is organized as the thing being looked at — which subordinates it.
Large area, no center; the small form has the only center there is.

**Tie-breaker:** focus. The landscape has size but no internal point; the figure is the only point.

---

## 3 — The man against the red field

**Strongest subject:** the man — lean, shirtless, the only form with edges, anatomy, and internal
detail in the frame.

**Runner-up (strong):** the saturated red field — it fills the entire background, the loudest and
largest single element, impossible to ignore.

**Why the red loses:** it has no internal form. The red is flat and uniform — no edges, no
structure, nothing inside it to read; it is intensity without shape. A subject has to *be* something
the eye can resolve into parts, and the red is one undifferentiated plane. Its only contour is the
silhouette where the man interrupts it — that edge belongs to the man, not the red. So even the
field's one shape is on loan from the figure. Loudness and area are not form.

**Tie-breaker:** internal form. The man can be resolved into parts; the red cannot be resolved at all.

---

## What breaks the tie

Across the three, the tie-breaker was different each time — independence, focus, internal form —
and no single one would have settled all three. That is the usable finding, stated narrowly: there
is no universal tie-breaker. The losing subject was strong in one currency (attention, area,
loudness) and lost in another that the *specific* frame made decisive. The evidence that breaks a
tie is particular to the image; the work is finding which currency that frame is paying in.
